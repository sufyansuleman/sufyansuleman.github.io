I am a computational biologist and geneticist who started at the bench. My path has run from protein science and molecular biology, through developmental biology, cancer genomics and experimental models, to statistical genetics and large-scale human data. This page begins with where I am heading and then traces how I got there, from Lahore, Pakistan, through two laboratories in Oulu, Finland, to Copenhagen and Aarhus, Denmark.

## Where I am heading

Before the story of how I navigated the path, it helps to know where it is leading, because the direction was shaped by every stage along the way.

My research has moved across biological scales, from **molecules to cells and tissues, from tissues to organisms, and from experimental systems to human populations**. Each stage gave me a different way of thinking about health, and each exposed the same limitation. Biological and clinical categories describe groups of people more easily than they explain the differences between them. That has become the question behind my current work. **How can we understand the biological variation between individuals that is hidden within population averages and clinical diagnoses?**

My work on insulin sensitivity showed that a single clinical phenotype can contain several underlying genetic architectures, with effects that differ across individuals and physiological contexts. It taught me to think about health less in terms of whether a person has a disease and more in terms of the components that shape individual risk and response.

I want to extend this by integrating **genetic variation with clinical phenotypes, biomarkers, nutrition and physical activity**, to study why people with apparently similar characteristics follow different trajectories of health and disease. Genetics is one measurable component of that system, not the whole explanation. The long-term aim is an **individual-level understanding of metabolic and mental health**, finding the meaningful sources of heterogeneity, the mechanisms behind them, and the ways genetic, physiological and behavioural information can be combined rather than studied apart.

The methods and tools I have built along the way, from statistical genetics and causal inference to open-source software and teaching, serve the same goal, **to make biological variation measurable, interpretable and reproducible, and to move health research beyond the average towards the person.**

That is the direction. What follows is how I got here, one field and one country at a time.

## Beginnings (2006-2013)

I took a BSc (Hons) in Biotechnology at Government College University Lahore, Pakistan, from 2006 to 2010, and then worked for three years as a quality control officer at a pharmaceutical company, my first exposure to checking and documenting a result before trusting it.

## Oulu, protein science and two labs (2014-2019)

### Studying protein science

In September 2014 I moved to Finland on a full scholarship for the International Master's Degree Programme in Protein Science and Biotechnology at the University of Oulu, majoring in Biochemistry, and graduated in May 2017. I flew from a city where summers reach plus 45 degrees into a winter of minus 35, and the first year carried real culture shock alongside that. Every year came with a residence permit renewal, a reminder that my right to stay was never entirely settled.

The coursework covered protein production and analysis, crystallographic methods, structural enzymology, protein folding and structure based drug discovery, together with systems biology, tumour cell biology, virology, yeast genetics and molecular biology. In the lab it meant recombinant expression, purification and downstream processing of proteins.

### Four labs

Biocenter Oulu ran its MSc research training through short rotations, and I went through four. First, in Seppo Vainio's lab, I optimised protocols for extracellular vesicle isolation, my first contact with the lab I would return to later. Then, in Peppi Karppinen's lab, I worked on HIF-1α expression and contributed to mouse experiments with Anna Laitakari, my first in vivo work. In Johanna Myllyharju's lab I worked on extracellular matrix proteins and collagen modifying enzymes. The fourth rotation, in Gong-Hong Wei's lab, became my move into cancer genomics and my MSc thesis, "Functional annotation of prostate cancer risk variants identifies RGS17 as a disease susceptibility gene", submitted in 2017.

### Cancer genomics with Gong-Hong Wei

After the MSc I stayed in the Wei lab as a research assistant, on the functional genomics of prostate and breast cancer susceptibility loci. My role changed paper by paper. On the 19q13 aggressive prostate cancer locus paper in *Cell* (2018) I am among the authors credited with assisting and performing experiments. On the SNPs-seq screening paper in *Nature Communications* (2018) I performed the experiments, running the high-throughput allele-specific protein-binding screen alongside EMSA and CRISPR/Cas9 editing. On the breast cancer exome-wide association study in *Cancer Research* (2018) my contribution was methodology, statistical analysis and manuscript writing. In the same period I wrote a first-author commentary on combined immunotherapy for advanced prostate cancer in the *Asian Journal of Urology* (2017).

### Back to the Vainio lab, Trisk 95

In the summer of 2018 I returned to Seppo Vainio's lab, with Nsrein Ali, on a project asking whether a skin protein could mirror systemic blood glucose. It was the most hands-on wet-lab work of my career. My credited contribution to the *Scientific Reports* paper (2020) was the Triadin, or Trisk 95, promoter luciferase reporter study, from cloning the promoter fragment to the dual-luciferase assay. The wider project ran from primary keratinocyte culture and imaging to a diabetic mouse model with glucose tolerance testing, which meant formal training in animal procedures under the European directive, training I still hold. I no longer want to be the person at the bench, and I am comfortable overseeing others there. The full list of techniques is in the inventory at the end of this page.

## Copenhagen, research assistant to PhD at CBMR (2019-2024)

This is the period I look back on as the most exciting stretch of my career so far.

### Arriving with no code

In September 2019 I joined the Novo Nordisk Foundation Center for Basic Metabolic Research, CBMR, at the University of Copenhagen in Denmark, as a research assistant with Niels Grarup and Torben Hansen. I arrived with essentially no coding skills. Everything I know now in R, GWAS pipelines and large genetic datasets was learned on the job in that group, on the Centre's cluster, first called Porus and later Esrum, and on the national resource Computerome. COVID arrived partway through and changed how the group worked, but the research on insulin resistance phenotypes continued.

### Three applications

Getting into a PhD took three tries. A fellowship application to the Novo Nordisk Foundation was rejected, a second to the Danish Diabetes Academy was rejected, and what I learned from both went into a third, to the Danish Independent Research Fund, DFF, which people said was the hardest of the three. It was the one that was funded. My PhD started in June 2021, co-funded by the Novo Nordisk Foundation and the DFF, with Niels Grarup as main supervisor, Tune Pers as co-supervisor and Torben Hansen as unofficial supervisor.

### The PhD

My thesis, "Genetics of insulin sensitivity and implications for T2D, obesity and cardiovascular disease", was built on a genome-wide association study of 21 insulin sensitivity indices in roughly 5,000 Danish individuals, with heritability and genetic and phenotypic correlations.

Three first-author papers followed. The first, in the *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism* (2024), showed that fasting and post-glucose insulin sensitivity indices have distinct genetic architectures, a finding that later became an interactive browser of T2D risk variants across 23 indices. The second, also in *JCEM* (2024), built three genetic risk scores for insulin resistance subtypes and validated them in the Holbæk paediatric cohort in Denmark. The third, in *Scientific Reports* (2025), examined where the genetics of obesity and insulin sensitivity overlap and diverge.

### Consortia, courses and Steno

Alongside the thesis I contributed to the MAGIC consortium's GWAS of glycaemic traits, published in *Nature Genetics* (2023), and to the SpiroMeta consortium's age-stratified lung function GWAS, still in preparation. Formal training in this period ran from basic statistics and GWAS analysis to the Wellcome Connecting Science course on computational systems biology, the Danish Diabetes Academy's cardiometabolic course and summer school, and all three of the Academy's R courses, two of which I later taught on in Copenhagen and one in Aarhus. In April 2024 I spent two months as a visiting researcher at Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen.

### The last months

In March 2024 Niels Grarup left CBMR for a position elsewhere. The group was winding down, with two other PhD students finishing in parallel, so the final months were thin on supervision. I asked Torben Hansen for two extra months to finish writing and look for what came next, and he gave me that time.

I had two realistic postdoc options. One was in Sweden, which would have meant commuting across the Øresund, an arrangement my residence situation did not allow. The other was in Aarhus, with Ditte Demontis and Anders Børglum, on the shared genetics of ADHD and obesity. I chose Aarhus.

### Choosing Aarhus

I moved to Aarhus in September 2024, returned to Copenhagen in November to defend the thesis, and the degree was conferred in December 2024. The overlap between obesity and insulin sensitivity that I had spent three years mapping had already raised a related question. Obesity's genetic links reach well beyond cardiometabolic disease, and ADHD is strongly comorbid with it. The tools I had built during the PhD, polygenic scoring, cross-cohort validation and consortium-scale GWAS, applied directly.

## Aarhus and the Broad, shared genetics of ADHD and obesity (2024-present)

Since September 2024 I have been a postdoctoral researcher in the Department of Biomedicine at Aarhus University, with Ditte Demontis and Anders Børglum, and an affiliate scientist at the NNF Center for Genomic Mechanisms of Disease at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The question at the centre is the shared genetic architecture and biology of ADHD and obesity, conditions that are epidemiologically linked but poorly understood at the genetic level.

This project is where I learned most of the statistical genetics toolkit I now use, largely from a standing start. Polygenic architecture and genetic correlation with MiXeR, LAVA and LDSC, cross-trait meta-analysis with ASSET and METASOFT, fine-mapping and gene prioritisation with FUMA, MAGMA, FINEMAP, PoPS and FLAMES, and polygenic scoring with SBayesRC feeding PheWAS across large phecode sets. I also began combining genetic results with LipocyteProfiler cellular imaging data from the Broad, to ask whether shared genetic risk shows up in cell morphology as well as in association statistics.

I built and lead a PhD course, Integrative Post-GWAS Methods, registered in the Aarhus University catalogue, and the ADHD and obesity work has been presented as a poster at the ESHG Annual Meeting in 2025, with the World Congress of Psychiatric Genetics in Glasgow, Scotland, to follow in September 2026.

## Building tools and teaching

Re-deriving the same insulin sensitivity formulas by hand during the PhD pushed me to write InsuSensCalc, which started as a lab tool and became a CRAN package. HealthMarkers and IsletCalc followed the same logic, and I contribute to MungeSumstats. The teaching grew the same way, from the Danish Diabetes Academy R courses and recurring genetics sessions for bachelor medical students in Aarhus, to the open courses and the PhD course I now run. The packages are described under Tools and the courses under Courses.

## The through-line

Looking back across Lahore, Oulu, Copenhagen and Aarhus, the pattern is less about any single disease area and more about repeatedly starting again. Quality control to protein biochemistry, wet-lab cancer genomics to statistical genetics, insulin sensitivity to ADHD, each move meaning a new country, lab or field, and its toolkit learned from zero.
